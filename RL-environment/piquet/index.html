Piquet

Thirty-two cards. Two players. One talon.

A deal is three games in one: swap cards with the talon, show off your best combinations, then fight for twelve tricks. Every part scores.

  1. Twelve cards each, 7 to Ace. The non-dealer, Elder, exchanges 1–5 cards with the talon; then Younger may exchange up to what is left.
  2. Declare your point (longest suit), sequence (cards in a row) and sets (three or four of a kind, 10 and up). Only the better hand in each scores.
  3. Play twelve tricks, no trumps, following suit. Leading scores 1, winning the opponent’s lead 1, the last trick 1, most tricks 10, all twelve 40.
  4. Reach 30 before your opponent scores and the bonus is huge: repique (+60) on declarations alone, or pique (+30) for Elder in play.
  5. Six deals make a partie. The higher total wins 100 plus the difference; finish under 100 and you are rubiconed: the winner adds both totals.

How the agent was trained

How the agent was trained

The AI you play is a small transformer, 9.1M parameters, that learned Piquet from scratch by playing against itself: first single deals, about 12 hours on one Mac GPU and roughly 420 million decisions over 13 million deals, then whole six-deal parties, about an hour more on the Mac and a desktop NVIDIA GPU over 170 thousand parties. It only sees what you see: its own hand, what it threw away and saw in the talon, what was declared and shown, the cards played and the score.

01 · Encoding

Every event is a token, and each one restates the 32 cards.

DEALSWAP 5SWAP 3exchange POINT 5·47GOODdeclarations K♠7♠a trick ?next

A Piquet hand changes twice (discard and draw) and then only shrinks, but what each player knows keeps shifting: Elder sees the talon cards it left, a winning declaration shows its cards, and each side’s discards stay secret. So every token states, for each of the 32 cards, where it is as far as the agent knows (its hand, its discards, seen in the talon, shown by the opponent, played) and restates the score, the tricks and the suits the opponent has failed to follow.

A small grid reads the cards as four suits of eight ranks: a convolution along each suit finds sequences and a pool over suits finds sets. A 16-layer causal transformer reads the stream, about 50 tokens a deal.

02 · Actions & objective

One card at a time. First the deal, then the partie.

Every action is a card, “stop”, or a yes/no declaration. The exchange (1,585 possible discards for Elder) is chosen one card at a time and closed with “stop”, each choice with the whole network behind it. Cards are scored by attention over their place on the grid, so the agent sees a card’s suit length, sequence and guards, not just its name.

The reward was first the point difference of a single deal. Then the agent played whole parties for the Rubicon payoff: 100 for the win plus the margin, or plus both totals when the loser stays under 100. Each deal is still a fresh context, and every token states the score: both running totals, the lead and the deal number. So it can trade points for the win or for the rubicon line when the score makes that worth it.

Both seats are the same network, trained with PPO. Its value head is the baseline (there is no separate critic) and predicts the partie’s payoff; side heads predict where every hidden card is, who wins each declaration, how many tricks each side takes and the fate of every card.

    Rules

    How to play Piquet

    Two players, 32 cards (7 to Ace in each suit, Ace high), twelve cards each and eight left face down as the talon. The non-dealer is Elder, the dealer Younger; the deal alternates.

    1 · The exchange

    Elder must discard 1 to 5 cards and draws as many from the top of the talon; Elder may look at any of its five it left behind. Then Younger may discard up to all the cards left and draws the same way. Discards stay face down. A hand without a King, Queen or Jack is carte blanche: it is shown and scores 10 before its owner exchanges.

    2 · Declarations

    Three categories, in order. Elder declares its best combination or says nothing; Younger then answers “good” (concedes), contests with a better one, or declares its own if Elder said nothing. Only the better combination scores, and its cards are shown. Equal point or sequence scores for neither. Saying nothing hides (sinks) a combination: it scores nothing.

    • Point: the most cards in one suit; equal lengths go to the higher total (A 11, K Q J 10 count 10, others face value). Scores 1 a card.
    • Sequence: three or more in a row in a suit: tierce, quart, quint and up. Longer wins, then the higher top card. The winner scores every sequence it holds: 3, 4, then 15–18 for five to eight cards.
    • Sets: three (trio, 3) or four (quatorze, 14) of a rank from the 10 up. Four beat three, then the higher rank. The winner scores every set.

    3 · The tricks

    Elder leads. Follow suit if you can; there are no trumps, so the higher card of the suit led wins. Leading a card scores 1, winning a trick your opponent led scores 1, and the last trick 1 more. Seven or more tricks win “the cards” for 10; all twelve (capot) score 40 instead.

    Pique and repique

    Repique (+60): reach 30 from carte blanche and declarations alone, before your opponent scores anything. Pique (+30, Elder only): reach 30 from declarations and play before Younger scores anything.

    The partie

    Six deals. The higher total wins 100 plus the difference; if the loser stayed under 100 (rubiconed), the winner scores 100 plus both totals.

    Single-option moves (a forced follow, the last trick) are played for you.
